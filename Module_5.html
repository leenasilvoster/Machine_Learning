Classification performance measures

Confusion matrix

Given m classes, a confusion matrix is a table of at least size m × m.

An entry CMi,j shows the number of tuples of class i (the row) that the classifier labelled as class j (the column). For a classifier to have good accuracy, most of the tuples should lie along the diagonal of the matrix, and the other entries should be close to zero. The table may also have extra rows or columns for totals or recognition rates per class.

With two classes, such as buys_computer = yes or no, we talk about positive tuples (the main class of interest, for example buys_computer = yes) and negative tuples (for example buys_computer = no).

Precision and Recall

Two metrics used to measure the performance of a retrieval system are precision and recall. Think of a search engine: it retrieves some pages, but only some of the pages that exist are truly relevant. Precision and recall each ask a different question about how well it did.

PrecisionHow many retrieved items are relevant?
RecallHow many relevant items are retrieved?

Precision

Precision is the fraction of relevant instances among the retrieved instances.

🤔Ask yourself: out of everything the system retrieved, how much was actually correct?

Precision is the number of correct instances retrieved divided by all retrieved instances. In the matrix, focus on the outlined Predicted Positive column: true positives and false positives. In a cancer example, it is the percentage of tuples labelled “cancer” that really are “cancer”.

📌 Example
A search returns 10 pages and 8 of them are relevant. Precision = 8 ÷ 10 = 0.8, which is 80%.

Recall

Recall is the fraction of relevant instances that were retrieved.

🤔Ask yourself: out of everything that was actually correct, how much did the system find?

Recall is the number of correct instances retrieved divided by all correct instances. In the matrix, focus on the outlined Actual Positive row: true positives and false negatives.

📌 Example
There are 20 relevant pages in total and the search finds 8 of them. Recall = 8 ÷ 20 = 0.4, which is 40%.

Accuracy

Accuracy is the number of correctly classified data instances divided by the total number of data instances.

🤔Ask yourself: out of all the predictions made, how many were correct?

Correct predictions are the true positives (TP) and true negatives (TN). Every prediction, correct or not, is counted in the denominator.

Accuracy = TN + TPTN + FP + TP + FN
= Correct predictionsTotal predictions
📌 Example
In the buys_computer table, 6,954 + 2,588 = 9,542 of the 10,000 tuples are classified correctly. Accuracy = 9,542 ÷ 10,000 = 95.42%.
⚠️ Be careful
Accuracy may not be a good measure when the dataset is imbalanced, that is, when the negative and positive classes have different numbers of instances. Suppose a classifier labels medical tuples as “cancer” or “not cancer”. An accuracy of 90% may make it seem quite accurate, but if only 3–4% of the tuples are actually “cancer”, 90% may not be acceptable: the classifier could be labelling only the “not cancer” tuples correctly. In such cases we also use sensitivity and specificity.

Accuracy from sensitivity and specificity

Accuracy is a function of sensitivity and specificity, where pos and neg are the numbers of positive and negative tuples:

accuracy = sensitivity ×pospos + neg+ specificity ×negpos + neg

F-Measure

The F1 score combines precision and recall into a single value, seeking a balance between the two.

🤔Ask yourself: how can I judge precision and recall together with just one number?

The F-score is a weighted average of precision and recall. The F1 score is their harmonic mean, so it is high only when both precision and recall are high.

F1 = 2 × Precision × RecallPrecision + Recall
📌 Example
Precision = 0.8 and Recall = 0.4. F1 = 2 × (0.8 × 0.4) ÷ (0.8 + 0.4) = 0.64 ÷ 1.2 ≈ 0.53. The simple average would be 0.6, so F1 is pulled down by the weaker score.
✅ When to use
F1 may be a better measure when we need a balance between precision and recall and the class distribution is uneven, that is, when there is a large number of actual negatives.

ROC curve and AUC

Rates from the confusion matrix

Sensitivity (True Positive Rate, TPR)

The fraction of correct predictions among all points in the positive class. It is simply the recall for the positive class, and it shows how well the classifier recognizes the positive tuples (for example, “cancer”).

TPR = recallP = TPTP + FN

Specificity (True Negative Rate, TNR)

The fraction of correct predictions among all points in the negative class. It is simply the recall for the negative class, and it shows how well the classifier recognizes the negative tuples (for example, “not cancer”).

TNR = specificity = recallN = TNFP + TN

False Negative Rate (FNR)

The fraction of positive points that the classifier wrongly predicts as negative.

FNR = FNTP + FN = 1 − sensitivity

False Positive Rate (FPR)

The fraction of negative points that the classifier wrongly predicts as positive.

FPR = FPFP + TN = 1 − specificity

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve

A ROC curve can only be used to assess classifiers that return a confidence score, or a probability, for their predictions.

📌 Examples
Logistic regression, neural networks and decision trees (and ensemble models based on decision trees) can all be assessed using ROC curves.

ROC curves commonly use the combination of the true positive rate (TPR) and the false positive rate (FPR), which are given as:

TPR = TPTP + FN  and  FPR = FPFP + TN

Area Under Curve (AUC)

The higher the area under the ROC curve (AUC), the better the classifier.

AUC > 0.5The classifier is better than a random classifier.
AUC < 0.5Something is wrong with your model.
AUC = 1A perfect classifier.
AUC valuesTest quality
0.9 – 1.0Excellent
0.8 – 0.9Very good
0.7 – 0.8Good
0.6 – 0.7Satisfactory
0.5 – 0.6Unsatisfactory

ROC curves are insensitive to class skew.

The TPR can be read as the probability of predicting a positive point as positive. The FPR can be read as the probability of predicting a negative point as positive. Neither depends on the ratio of the positive class size to the negative class size.

✅ Good to know
This is a desirable property. The ROC curve stays essentially the same whether the classes are balanced (about the same number of points) or skewed (one class has many more points than the other).

Evaluating the accuracy of a classifier

Evaluating the accuracy of a classifier or predictor

All the common techniques for assessing accuracy are based on randomly sampled partitions of the given data.

That is, the data are randomly partitioned into two independent sets, a training set and a test set. The four common techniques are:

HoldoutOne random split into a training set and a test set.
Random subsamplingThe holdout method repeated k times.
Cross-validationEvery fold takes a turn as the test set.
BootstrapSamples are drawn from the data with replacement.

Holdout

In the holdout method, the given data are randomly partitioned into two independent sets, a training set and a test set.

Typically, two-thirds of the data are allocated to the training set and the remaining one-third to the test set. The training set is used to derive the model, and the accuracy is estimated with the test set.

Random subsampling

Random subsampling is a variation of the holdout method in which the holdout method is repeated k times.

  1. In each iteration, randomly select a fixed number of test tuples without replacement, so a tuple does not repeat, and estimate the accuracy.
  2. Take the overall accuracy estimate as the average of the accuracies obtained from the k iterations.
Overall accuracy = 1kkΣi = 1accuracyi

For prediction, we can take the average of the predictor error rates instead.

Bootstrapping

The bootstrap method is a resampling technique used to estimate statistics on a population by sampling a dataset with replacement.

🤔Ask yourself: how can I estimate something about a population when I only have one dataset?

The bootstrap method estimates quantities about a population by averaging the estimates from multiple small data samples.

How it works

  1. Generate t samples of size n by sampling from the dataset D with replacement. The same point can be chosen more than once, so each sample Di is different.
  2. For each sample Di, run the same algorithm.

Bootstrap for estimating accuracy

Unlike holdout, random subsampling and cross-validation, the bootstrap method samples the given training tuples uniformly with replacement. Each time a tuple is selected, it is equally likely to be selected again, because it is put back into the pool. The tuples that never make it into the bootstrap sample form the test set.

A commonly used method is the .632 bootstrap. On average, 63.2% of the original tuples end up in the bootstrap sample and the remaining 36.8% form the test set (see the probability under Bagging in detail). The sampling is repeated k times, and the accuracy of the model M is estimated by combining the test-set and training-set accuracies:

Acc(M) = 1kkΣi = 1(0.632 × Acc(Mi)test + 0.368 × Acc(Mi)train)

Here Acc(Mi)test is the accuracy of the model from sample i on its test set, and Acc(Mi)train is its accuracy on the original training data.

✅ Why use it
Bootstrapping improves the stability of machine learning algorithms, and combined with aggregation, as in bagging, it helps reduce overfitting.

Cross validation

Cross validation is a resampling technique that evaluates a model by repeatedly training it on part of the data and testing it on the part that was held out.

🤔Ask yourself: how can I check how well my model will work on new data without wasting any of my data?

In k-fold cross validation, training and testing are performed k times. In iteration i, the fold Di is reserved as the test (validation) set, and the remaining folds are used together to train the model.

How it works

  1. Randomly partition the initial data into k mutually exclusive subsets, called folds, D1, D2, …, Dk, each of approximately equal size.
  2. In each iteration i = 1 to k, train the model on the other k − 1 folds and test it on Di. Record the score as Performancei.
  3. Average the k scores to get the final performance estimate.

📐 Estimating accuracy and error
Classification: the accuracy is the total number of correct classifications from the k iterations divided by the total number of tuples in the initial data. With folds of equal size, this equals the average of the k scores above.
Prediction: the error estimate is the total loss from the k iterations divided by the total number of initial tuples.
📌 Example
A dataset has 100 instances and k = 5. Each fold holds 20 instances. In every iteration the model trains on 80 instances and is validated on the other 20, so each instance is used for validation exactly once.
k-foldThe standard method shown above. Common choices are k = 5 or k = 10.
Stratified k-foldEach fold keeps the same class proportions as the full dataset, which helps when the classes are imbalanced.
Leave-one-outThe extreme case where k equals the number of instances, so each fold is a single instance.
✅ Why use it
Every data point is used for both training and validation, and the averaged score is a more reliable estimate than a single train/test split. It also helps reveal overfitting.

Ensemble methods

Ensemble methods

Ensemble learning is a general meta approach to machine learning that seeks better predictive performance by combining the predictions from multiple models.

🤔Ask yourself: if one model is not accurate enough, can several models working together do better?
Baggingshort for bootstrap aggregating
Fits many decision trees on different samples of the same dataset and averages the predictions.
Boostingsequential
Adds ensemble members one at a time, each correcting the predictions made by the earlier models, and outputs a weighted average of the predictions.
Stackingcombining different models
Fits many different model types on the same data and uses another model to learn how to best combine their predictions.

Voting

Voting is the simplest procedure for combining the outcomes of several learning algorithms.

Suppose there are L learners and x is a test instance. Voting follows the same two steps for classification, whatever the number of classes:

  1. Each learner assigns a label to x, and that label receives one vote.
  2. The votes are counted, and the label with the majority of votes is assigned to x.
Binary classificationThe class labels are +1 and −1, so every learner votes for one of the two.
Multi-class classificationThere are n class labels C1, C2, …, Cn, and every learner votes for one of them.
RegressionThere are no labels to count. Each learner predicts a value, and the predictions are combined with weights.

Regression: weighted voting

Each learner predicts a value for the variable y. The outputs y1, y2, …, yL are each multiplied by a weight and added:

y = w1y1 + w2y2 + … + wLyL
wj ≥ 0,  j = 1, 2, …, Landw1 + w2 + … + wL = 1
📌 Example
Classification: five learners vote +1, +1, −1, +1, −1. The label +1 gets 3 votes and −1 gets 2, so x is labelled +1.
Regression: three learners predict 10, 20 and 30 with weights 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2. Then y = 0.5×10 + 0.3×20 + 0.2×30 = 17.

Bagging in detail

A learning algorithm is unstable if small changes in the training set cause a large difference in the learner it produces.

Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) is designed for such unstable algorithms. Starting from the initial dataset D:

  1. Draw k random samples D1, D2, …, Dk from D with replacement, as in bootstrapping. Some instances repeat and some are left out.
  2. Train a different model on each subset, using an unstable learning procedure for the base learners.
  3. Take the average of the outputs of all the models.

How many instances are picked?

In a single draw, a particular instance is not picked with probability 1 − 1/n. The probability that it is still not picked after n draws is:

(1 − 1n)n ≈ e−1 = 0.368

So about 63% of the instances in the dataset occur in each subset. For a dataset of 1000 instances, roughly 632 different instances appear in every sample.

✅ Why it works
Individual learners are unstable and have high variance. By combining them, the variance is reduced.

Boosting in detail

Boosting is an iterative procedure that combines weak learners to get a strong learner.

🤔Ask yourself: can many simple, slightly better than random, classifiers be turned into one very accurate classifier?

At the start, all n instances are given equal weights. Then, on each iteration t:

  1. Learn a hypothesis ht. Some instances are labelled correctly and some wrongly.
  2. Increase the weights of the wrongly labelled instances, so the next learner pays more attention to them.
  3. Work out the strength of the hypothesis and add the weak learner ht(x) to the final model.

The algorithm step by step

🧾 In plain words
εt is the total weight of the wrongly classified instances, which is the error. αt is the strength of the hypothesis: the smaller the error, the larger the strength. For a wrongly labelled instance ht(x)y(x) = −1, so its weight is multiplied by eαt and grows. Z is a normalising constant that keeps the weights adding up to 1.

In the first iteration the classifier makes mistakes: 2 yellow dots are misclassified. In the next iteration the boosting algorithm focuses on the misclassified items, because their weights have been increased.

Putting it together: the final classifier

We keep combining the weak learners h(x). Finally, we get the model H(x), which is a strong learner. It is a linear combination of the hypotheses, each weighted by its strength:

H(x) = sign(TΣt = 1αt ht(x))

We just have to combine these weak classifiers!

Bias–variance decomposition

Bias-Variance decomposition

Bias–variance decomposition splits the expected prediction error of a model into three parts: bias, variance and irreducible noise.

🤔Ask yourself: why can a model that fits the training data very well still make poor predictions on new data?
BiasHow far the model's average prediction is from the true value. High bias means the model is too simple, so it underfits.
VarianceHow much the predictions change when the model is trained on different training sets. High variance means the model is too sensitive to its training data, so it overfits.
NoiseRandomness in the data itself. No model can remove it, so it is called irreducible error.

The decomposition

Suppose the true relationship is y = f(x) + ε, where the noise ε has mean zero and variance σ². Let f̂(x) be the model learned from a random training set. For squared error, the expected error at a point x is:

Expected error = Bias² + Variance + Noise
E[(y − f̂(x))²] = (E[f̂(x)] − f(x))² + E[(f̂(x) − E[f̂(x)])²] + σ²

E[ ] is the average over many possible training sets. The first term is the squared bias, the second is the variance, and σ² is the noise.

The bias–variance trade-off

Making a model more complex lowers its bias but raises its variance. The aim is the complexity at which the total error, the sum of the three parts, is smallest.

📌 Example
Fitting a straight line to data that follow a curve gives high bias: the line is too simple, so it misses the pattern. Fitting a very high-degree polynomial that passes through every training point gives high variance: the curve changes a lot whenever the training points change.

How to spot and fix each problem

ProblemTraining errorTest errorWhat helps
High bias (underfitting)HighHigh, close to the training errorA more complex model, better features, less regularisation
High variance (overfitting)LowMuch higher than the training errorMore data, a simpler model or regularisation, bagging
Good balanceLowClose to the training errorKeep the model; check it with cross-validation
🔗 Link to this module
Bagging averages many unstable, high-variance learners, which reduces variance. Boosting combines weak, high-bias learners step by step, which mainly reduces bias. Cross-validation estimates the test error, so it helps to choose the model complexity.
